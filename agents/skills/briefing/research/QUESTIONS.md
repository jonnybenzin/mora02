# Question catalogue — research brief

For jobs that start with "find out", "what is there on", "compare these" or
"is X worth it".

A research job rarely fails because too little searching was done. It fails
because the question was not sharp, because nobody said which sources count, or
because nobody knew when enough is enough. These eleven questions are built
against exactly those three ways of failing.

Ask them one at a time, in your own words. Skip any that an earlier answer has
already answered.

---

## 1. The question

What exactly do you want to know? In one sentence, shaped so that an answer
could fit it.

*Push back if it is a subject rather than a question. "Something about e-bikes"
is a subject. "Which mid-drive motors last beyond 20,000 km" is a question.*

## 2. The decision behind it

What will you do with the answer? What will you decide or change afterwards?

*This question steers the depth more than any other. Someone getting their
bearings needs three good sources. Someone about to buy needs prices and
evidence.*

## 3. What you already know, and the constraints

What do you know already, and what may you have checked yourself? And **what
does it have to run on** — hardware, environment, tools already in use?

*The first part stops half the research from turning up things you have known
for ages — the commonest reason a result disappoints.*

*The second part matters more than it sounds, and was forgotten in the first
version: for "what should I take / install / buy", the constraints sift harder
than any judgement of quality. How much VRAM, which operating system, which
budget, which existing setup — a candidate that does not fit is not a
candidate, however good it is. If the answer is "no idea", ask about the
constraints anyway: nearly everyone knows those, even people who do not know
the products.*

## 4. Angles to search from

Which directions come to mind? Technical terms, product names, manufacturers,
opposing positions, English terms?

*Valuable even when incomplete: three angles named are three searches nobody
else would have run.*

## 5. What counts as a source

What convinces you — manufacturer figures, independent tests, trade articles,
forums, news? And what expressly does **not** count?

*Almost never volunteered, and it settles every later argument about the
result. Do not stop at "anything really" — that is not an answer, it is the
absence of one. Come at it backwards: **"What would NOT convince you?"**
Someone who cannot say what counts can nearly always say what does not.*

## 6. How current it has to be

How old may a figure be? Is there a date before which something is worthless?

*For prices, legal positions and software this is often the most important
restriction — and the one where silent errors come from, because an old result
looks exactly like a new one.*

## 7. Region and language

Which market or country does the question apply to, and which languages may be
searched in?

## 8. Scope

How deep? An overview with three sources, or a thorough round?

*Ask honestly, even if the answer is "don't know" — then it is an overview, and
more can be added later.*

## 9. Shape of the result

What should the answer look like — a recommendation, a comparison table, a list
with links, a summary?

*For a comparison, ask straight away which columns — and wait for the answer.
Proposing columns and then writing them into the document as agreed is the
quiet version of the same mistake: what gets compared is then something nobody
asked for. A "comparison" without criteria is not a job.*

*If the question is skipped it is still answerable: what someone wants to pull
out of a comparison follows from what they are deciding — someone who wants to
install a model needs a recommendation and a table, and the columns follow from
the subject. That should then be assumed and marked as assumed, not left open.*

## 10. What the question is not

What expressly does **not** belong to it? What should not be allowed to sprawl?

## 11. How you would know it is answered

When would you say: that will do?

*The hardest question and the most valuable. Someone who can answer it has a
job; someone who cannot has a wish. If there is no answer, write that down
verbatim — then whoever does the research knows they have to decide for
themselves when to stop.*
